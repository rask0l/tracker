01 — What's actually happening overnight

Memory consolidation happens primarily during sleep, and the two main stages do different jobs. Slow-wave sleep consolidates declarative memory, the explicit stuff: what a second-order injection is, which parameter took the payload, the syntax you looked up. REM sleep handles non-declarative memory, the procedural stuff you can't quite put into words: the feel of knowing where to poke at an app, pattern recognition, the thing that makes an experienced tester faster than a checklist.

Mechanically, during non-REM sleep the coupling of slow oscillations, spindles and sharp-wave ripples transfers memory from the hippocampus out to the cortex, which is what moves something from "I did this today" to "I know this." REM theta activity then handles integration, abstraction and emotional tagging.

Human studies find that the product of slow-wave and REM percentages predicts overnight consolidation better than either stage alone. They're complementary, not redundant. A short night doesn't shave a little off both evenly, it disproportionately cuts the later REM-heavy cycles, which is exactly the part doing the procedural and pattern work that hacking runs on.

02 — The ADHD multiplier

This is the finding that should change your behavior more than anything else here. In recent work on young adults with ADHD: when well rested, they performed comparably to neurotypical controls. After sleep deprivation, the ADHD group's performance declined on commission errors, omission errors, reaction time and reaction time variability, while the control group was essentially unaffected.

Read that again. Same deprivation, same task, and one group absorbs it while the other falls apart. Sleep deprivation is not a flat tax, and if you have ADHD you are paying a much higher rate than the person next to you who "also only got five hours."

The mechanism lines up: sleep loss disrupts dopaminergic and noradrenergic signaling and reduces prefrontal activity, which are precisely the systems already running with less headroom in ADHD. There's no reserve to eat into. So the day after a short night, the focus problem you're blaming on discipline is substantially a sleep problem wearing a costume.

03 — Sleep and mood run both directions

The relationship between sleep and depression is bidirectional, and this is the part most people get backwards. Poor sleep isn't only a symptom of feeling bad. Longitudinal studies identify insomnia as an independent risk factor for developing new or recurrent depression, which reframes sleep problems as a predictive early sign rather than a side effect.

The association is not small: people with insomnia are reported to be around ten times more likely to have depression and seventeen times more likely to have anxiety than the general population. Sleep loss also directly reduces the ability to regulate emotion, which is why everything feels more unmanageable on no sleep, including things that were fine yesterday.

The useful half of this: because it runs both ways, sleep is a lever, not just a readout. Treating insomnia is currently seen as a genuine opportunity to prevent depressive episodes rather than merely clean up after them. CBT for insomnia is the evidence-backed route, and combined treatment beats medication alone in the trials. This is one of the few things on a bad stretch that is both actionable and worth actual professional help.

04 — The gym half, honestly

Worth being accurate here rather than alarmist, because the popular version oversells it. One bad night will not undo your training. A single night of poor sleep has little effect on maximal strength, though it can raise perceived fatigue and hurt repeated efforts. Total sleep deprivation after muscle damage doesn't even delay strength recovery directly, it shifts the inflammatory and hormonal response around it.

The real cost is chronic, not acute:

  • 01Protein synthesis. A single night of total deprivation measurably reduced muscle protein synthesis and altered remodelling markers. Repair happens during rest, so the adaptation you trained for is executed while you sleep, not while you lift.
  • 02Hormones. 24 hours or more of deprivation lowered serum testosterone; partial deprivation did not. Chronic restriction is the problem, lowering testosterone, disturbing growth hormone rhythm and raising cortisol.
  • 03Net direction. A higher cortisol-to-testosterone ratio shifts you toward a catabolic state, which makes building muscle harder over months. Not a single session, a slow tax on every session.

05 — What this means for the Loop

Three practical consequences, none of them requiring extra willpower:

  • 01Sleep is not slack in the schedule. Cutting an hour of sleep to add an hour of study doesn't trade one for one, it removes the process that converts the study into retained skill. You end up paying for the hour twice.
  • 02A bad night should change the next day's plan, not just be endured. Given the ADHD multiplier, a short night is a legitimate reason to drop to the floor version of a study day rather than the ceiling one. That's a correct response to conditions, not a failure of discipline.
  • 03Log it, since you already do. The gym log already records hours slept. That column is quietly the most predictive one in the file. When a week goes badly, check it before concluding anything about motivation.

The neuroplasticity piece argues that the brain rewires around what gets used repeatedly. This is the other half of that claim: the rewiring itself is scheduled overnight. Practice writes the to-do list, sleep does the work.
