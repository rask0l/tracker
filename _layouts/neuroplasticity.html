Neuroplasticity

Notes to self · why the Loop is built the way it is

The brain only rewires around what actually gets used, repeatedly. That single fact is the reason every rule on the Loop page looks the way it does. This is the reasoning written out.

01 — The mechanism

Skill acquisition happens in two stages. First, fast functional reorganization: existing circuits get recruited and strengthened within days. Second, and only if the skill keeps getting practiced, slower structural change: more myelin, denser gray matter, physically different wiring. The first stage is cheap and reversible. The second is what actually sticks, and it only shows up under repetition.

Underneath both, the same handful of mechanisms keep showing up across skill-learning research: long-term potentiation strengthening the synapses actually in use, dopaminergic signaling in the corticostriatal loop, BDNF-driven growth, cerebellar correction of errors. None of it is exotic. It's all downstream of one thing: use.

02 — No permanent reserve

The finding that mattered most when I looked into this: the cognitive benefit of learning tracks ongoing engagement, not a one-time structural deposit. There's no evidence you "bank" a skill permanently just because you once got good at it.

A module marked done in the roadmap is not a module that's finished. It's a module that will decay on a normal forgetting curve unless it gets touched again. "Done" means learned to standard once, not learned forever.

03 — The ADHD wiring

ADHD brains show dopamine-driven plasticity: they rewire readily around high-interest, high-reward activity, and rewire poorly around sustained, low-reward effort. This isn't a discipline problem, it's a difference in which activities actually trigger the plasticity machinery in the first place. Fighting it head-on (forcing the boring part first, on willpower alone) is working against the wiring instead of with it.

The fix isn't motivation, it's sequencing: put the high-interest task first (attacking a lab, hyperfocus-friendly), and let the low-reward task (the writeup, the report language) ride the leftover engagement right after, while the circuit is still warm. That's why Learn comes before Consolidate in the Loop, not the other way around.

04 — What actually moves the needle

  • 01Repetition frequency over session length. Use-dependent plasticity responds to how often a circuit fires, not how long one session runs. Short and daily beats long and rare — the actual reason for "two bugs a week," not a cram.
  • 02Retrieval over rereading. Reconstructing what you learned from memory drives more structural change per minute than passively reviewing it. Closed-book first, then patch gaps — never the reverse.
  • 03Exercise widens the window. Training spikes BDNF for roughly two hours afterward, meaning the brain is measurably more plastic in that window. A study block that lands inside it is worth more than the same block hours away from it.
  • 04Sleep is the consolidation step, not the recovery step. Structural change (myelination, synaptic pruning) largely happens during sleep after practice, not during it. Cutting sleep to fit in more study cuts the step that locks the gain in.
  • 05Age isn't the constraint here. General skill-learning plasticity doesn't meaningfully decline until well into the 30s–40s, and even then gradually. At 22, the only real variable is consistency, not a closing window.

05 — Where this shows up on the Loop

Rules 05 and 06 on the Loop page came directly out of this: train before you study to land inside the BDNF window, and cycle old "done" modules back into warm-up so plasticity that isn't reused doesn't quietly reverse. The rest of the Loop's shape (Learn before Consolidate, closed-book writeups, short daily sessions over cramming) was arrived at independently but turns out to match the mechanism almost exactly.
