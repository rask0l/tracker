01 — How long it actually takes

The number people quote is 66 days, from Lally's 2010 study where 96 volunteers picked a daily behaviour and tracked it for 12 weeks. The real finding is messier and more useful than the headline: 66 days was the median, with a range of 18 to 254, and automaticity climbed along an asymptotic curve rather than flipping on at some threshold. Simple actions hit automaticity faster than elaborate ones.

The part that never makes it into the productivity blogs: only about 48% of participants succeeded at all. So 66 days isn't a promise, it's a median among the people it worked for. Anyone quoting it as a guarantee is quoting a number they didn't read the paper for.

02 — The ADHD number is different

Clinical estimates for ADHD put habit automaticity closer to 3 to 5 months, roughly 106 to 154 days. The mechanism is the same one from the neuroplasticity piece: habit formation runs on a dopamine feedback loop, and PET imaging shows reduced dopamine receptor and transporter density in the nucleus accumbens and midbrain, the regions handling motivation and reward. Weaker signal per repetition means more repetitions needed.

The harder finding, and the more useful one: for ADHD brains some routines may never become fully automatic. Things done daily for years can still require conscious effort. External systems, visible reminders, timers, body doubling, a tracker you actually open, are not training wheels you eventually take off. They're permanent infrastructure, and treating them as a crutch to outgrow is how people end up unsupported and confused about why it stopped working.

03 — Missing a day costs almost nothing

This is the empirical centre of the whole piece. In Lally's data, a missed opportunity reduced automaticity by less than half a point on the scale, and scores recovered quickly. Missing one day did not materially affect habit formation. The curve dips and resumes.

So the intuition that a broken streak undoes accumulated progress is not supported. The progress lives in the accumulated repetitions, which don't get deleted because Tuesday didn't happen. Nothing was reset. There is no counter that went to zero except the one you made up.

04 — What actually ends a run

If one missed day is nearly free, why do runs collapse? Because of what happens next, and it's well documented. The abstinence violation effect describes exactly this: someone breaks a rule they set for themselves, concludes the break means total failure, and reasons that they may as well abandon the whole thing. The literature calls the follow-on the "what the hell" response.

The phrasing that matters: the initial slip is not the catastrophe. The avalanche of shame and catastrophic thinking after it is what turns a minor setback into a full relapse. Risk is highest for people holding all-or-nothing goals, because under that framing a single miss is indistinguishable from total collapse, so there's nothing left worth protecting.

Look at the log in cwes.yml. One entry, dated 30 July, then an eight-week gap. That gap was not caused by eight weeks of deciding not to study. It was one missed stretch, followed by the conclusion that the run was dead, which made every subsequent day feel like it didn't count either. That's the effect above, in this file, with a date on it.

The documented counter is unglamorous: treat a lapse as data rather than a verdict, and use self-compassion deliberately, not as a nice idea but because shame is the mechanism that converts one miss into twenty. CBT works here specifically because it targets the all-or-nothing thinking that gives the effect its power.

05 — Consistency beats intensity, measurably

Separate literature, same conclusion. The spacing effect shows distributed practice produces better long-term retention than massed practice even when total practice time is held constant. Less intense training spread over more days beats concentrated training. Concretely: four 50-minute sessions outperform one 4-hour session.

The reason is that a small amount of forgetting between sessions is a feature. Retrieval becomes slightly harder, which forces reconstruction, which is what strengthens retention. Cramming removes the difficulty and therefore removes the benefit.

This is the actual argument against making up lost time in one heroic weekend. It isn't that you'd be tired. It's that the same hours, compressed, buy less retained skill than they would spread out.

06 — What this means for the Loop

  • 01Rule 01 was already right. "Miss a day? Resume, don't double up. The streak breaking isn't the run breaking." That's the abstinence violation effect, pre-empted. Keep it at the top, because it's the rule the other five depend on.
  • 02Count repetitions, not streaks. The roadmap bar measures modules finished, which doesn't reset when a week goes badly. That's the correct metric and it was a good instinct. Never add a streak counter to this site.
  • 03Keep a floor, not just a target. A defined minimum that still counts as a successful day is the structural defence against all-or-nothing thinking. If the only outcomes are "full session" and "failure", the effect above has an opening.
  • 04The debt cap was right too. Wednesday's catch-up is capped at ~2h and resets that night, never rolling forward. Unbounded debt is how a bad week becomes a quit, and spacing says the compressed makeup session was worth less anyway.
  • 05Expect 3 to 5 months, not 3 weeks. If the rhythm still feels effortful in November, that's the documented timeline for an ADHD brain, not evidence it isn't working.
